Crane types we supply

All terrain cranes

An all terrain crane is a mobile crane with a telescopic boom on a multi-axle carrier that drives on roads and rough ground.

Explore

Crawler cranes

A crawler crane is a lifting machine mounted on steel tracks, with a lattice or telescopic boom.

Explore

Truck mounted cranes

A truck mounted crane is a crane boom fixed on a standard truck chassis.

Explore

Tower cranes

A tower crane is a tall fixed crane with a vertical mast and a horizontal jib, used to lift building materials across a construction site.

Explore

EOT cranes and overhead cranes

An EOT crane (electric overhead travelling crane) runs on rails high in a workshop or yard and lifts loads with a hoist on a moving bridge.

Explore

Rough terrain cranes

A rough terrain crane is a compact mobile crane with four large wheels, a single cab and a telescopic boom, built to work on uneven, unpaved ground.

What is a load chart and why does it matter?

A load chart lists how much a crane can lift at each radius and boom length. A crane marketed as 200 tons lifts far less at long reach. Always compare the chart for your actual radius, not the headline capacity.

When you send an enquiry, include the heaviest lift, its radius, the required hook height and the lift duration. That lets us shortlist machines that fit the job on paper.

How do I choose between an all terrain and a crawler crane?

Choose an all terrain crane when you move between sites or need fast set-up on firm ground. Choose a crawler crane when the lift is heavy, the ground is soft or uneven, or the crane stays on one site for weeks. Compare the pages for all terrain cranes and crawler cranes.

When does a tower crane make sense?

A tower crane suits tall buildings and long projects where you need to serve the full footprint repeatedly. For a one-off heavy lift, a mobile crane is usually more practical. Read about tower cranes and truck mounted cranes.

What do I check before buying a crane?

  • Load chart and boom configuration match your lifts
  • Working hours, year and service history
  • Structural condition of boom, outriggers and slew ring
  • Wire rope, hoist and brake condition
  • Safety systems: load moment indicator, anti-two-block, limit switches
  • Documents required for import in your country

What applications use cranes?

Typical applications include steel erection, power plant and refinery maintenance, bridge and road projects, precast installation, wind and solar equipment, port and yard handling, and heavy machinery relocation.

Crane terms explained

Radius and capacity

Radius is the horizontal distance from the crane centre to the load. Capacity falls as radius grows, which is why a crane named for its maximum tonnage lifts far less at long reach.

Boom, jib and counterweight

The boom is the main arm, a jib is an extension at its tip, and the counterweight balances the load. A crane with the wrong counterweight fitted has a different capacity from the one on its brochure.

Outriggers and ground bearing

Outriggers are the hydraulic legs that level a mobile crane. They spread the load, but the ground under them must still carry the pressure, so mats and ground checks are part of every lift plan.

Load moment indicator

This is the onboard computer that compares the actual load and radius with the load chart and warns the operator near the limit. A working indicator is a basic requirement on a modern crane.

An all terrain crane is a purpose-built carrier with multiple axes and all-wheel steering, built for rough sites. A truck mounted crane places a boom on a truck chassis, which is simpler and often lighter in capacity.
