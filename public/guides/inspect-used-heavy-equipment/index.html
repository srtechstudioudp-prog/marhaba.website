How to inspect used heavy equipment before you buy

To inspect used heavy equipment, check the engine, hydraulics, undercarriage or tyres, structure and welds, and the documents, then compare the hour meter with the real wear. Ask for pictures, oil sample results and a test run video. Do not buy from the hour meter alone.

Two excavators have the same age and hours. One was kept in a quarry and the other on a pipeline job in sand. After a few minutes under the cab the difference is obvious: pins, cylinders and tracks tell a different story than the hour meter.

Why does an inspection matter?

Used heavy equipment is sold on trust, but you often buy from another country. An inspection replaces trust with evidence. It tells you what repairs to budget for and whether the price is fair.

Our process is that you order after reviewing inspection reports and pictures, so ask for the points below to be covered.

The five-area inspection checklist

AreaWhat to checkRed flags
EngineCold start, smoke colour, oil condition, leaks, blow-byHeavy white or blue smoke, metal in oil, overheating
HydraulicsPump response, cylinder rods, hoses, leaks, driftSlow movement, scored rods, constant top-ups
Undercarriage / tyresTrack wear, rollers, sprockets, tyre depth and cutsHeavily worn links, loose tracks, cracked tyres
StructureWelds, cracks, pins, bushes, slew ring, bucketRepaired cracks, excess play, bent parts
Cab and electricsControls, gauges, lights, safety systemsWarning lights, tampered hour meter, missing guards

How do I check the real working hours?

The hour meter can be wrong or replaced. Compare it with wear: pedals, seat, joystick grips, pin play and track condition. Ask for service records and the serial number plate. A low-hour claim on a heavily worn machine should raise questions.

What should I check on an excavator?

  • Swing bearing play and noise
  • Boom and stick welds and pins
  • Cylinder rods for scoring and leaks
  • Track chain stretch and sprocket wear
  • Pump pressure and travel speed
  • Quick coupler and auxiliary hydraulics

What should I check on a wheel loader or dozer?

  • Transmission shifts and torque converter heat
  • Articulation joint pins and bushes (loaders)
  • Final drives and axle seals
  • Bucket or blade cutting edge and wear plates
  • Tyres (loaders) or tracks and rollers (dozers)
  • Brake performance and parking brake

What should I check on a crane?

  • Boom sections, pads and extension condition
  • Wire rope, hook block and sheaves
  • Slew ring and outrigger cylinders
  • Load moment indicator and limit switches working
  • Load chart present and matching the boom configuration
  • Service and inspection records

What documents should I ask for?

  • Serial number plate photo and matching documents
  • Service history and maintenance records
  • Load chart and manuals (cranes)
  • Previous inspection or certification reports
  • Export or de-registration documents where required
  • Commercial invoice and packing list

Three inspection mistakes to avoid

Judging by paint and cleanliness

A freshly painted, steam-cleaned machine can hide leaks, cracks and repairs. Ask for photos of the machine before and after cleaning, and of the welds and undersides.

Accepting a short test

Some faults only appear when oil is hot or the machine is loaded. Ask for a video of a full working cycle after warm-up, with all functions used together.

Skipping the serial number match

The plate on the machine, the engine and the documents should agree. A mismatch can mean stolen, rebuilt or mixed-up equipment.

What are the warning signs that mean walk away?

  • The seller refuses photos or a video of a test run
  • Serial numbers do not match the documents
  • Fresh paint hiding welds and cracks
  • Price far below market with no explanation
  • Pressure to pay before any inspection

Should I order an oil sample or third-party inspection?

For high-value machines, yes. Oil analysis can reveal wear metals and contamination. A third-party inspector can verify condition at the yard. Tell us if you want one arranged and we will include it in the discussion with the source.

Frequently asked questions

What is the most important thing to inspect?

It depends on the machine, but the engine, hydraulics and undercarriage or tyres are the largest cost items, so check these first.

Can I trust the hour meter?

Not alone. Compare it with the wear on controls, pins and tracks, and ask for service records.

What is blow-by?

It is combustion gas leaking past the pistons into the crankcase. Heavy blow-by indicates engine wear.

How do I check hydraulic condition?

Test the speed and strength of each function, look for leaking cylinders and hoses, and ask about oil changes.

How do I check undercarriage wear?

Measure track link and sprocket wear against the manufacturer's limits, and check rollers and idlers for leaks and flat spots.

Do I need an oil analysis?

It is useful for high-value machines. It can show wear metals and contamination in engine and hydraulic oil.

What documents should come with a used crane?

Load chart, manuals, service history and any inspection or certification reports.
